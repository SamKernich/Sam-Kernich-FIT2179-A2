Great Barrier Reef

Introduction

The Great Barrier Reef is the world's largest coral reef ecosystem, stretching over 2,300 km along the northeast coast of Australia. Home to thousands of species of marine life, it is one of the seven natural wonders of the world and a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Yet decades of rising ocean temperatures, agricultural runoff, and coastal development have pushed the reef to a critical threshold. This dashboard presents an insight into the current state of the reef, the trends driving its decline, and the urgent need for conservation action to preserve this irreplaceable ecosystem.

GBR Affected Regions

The Australian Institute of Marine Science (AIMS) monitors coral cover across 64 key reefs spanning the length of the Great Barrier Reef. Over the last 2 decades, the reef has gone through several bleaching periods, with the most severe impacts observed in the northern and central sections. A bleaching event occurs when the water temperature increases to the point that the coral have to expel the colourful algae (zooxanthellae) that live in their tissues, causing them to turn white. If stressful conditions persist, the damage is irreversible and the coral can die. On the map, the colour gradient represents the average live coral cover percentage across all surveyed reefs in each region, with cooler shades indicating healthier coral cover and warmer shades indicating more severe degradation. Fortunately, some regions of the reef have increases in live coral as the water temperatures have returned to equilibrium before the coral have died, however this is not the case across the whole reef.

Over 50% of shallow-water coral cover has been lost since the 1980s, with mass bleaching events recorded in 1998, 2002, 2006, 2016, 2017, 2020, 2022, and 2024. Unfortunately, the frequency of bleaching events has increased to the point where many reefs are now experiencing back-to-back bleaching, with insufficient time for recovery between episodes. The 2016–2017 bleaching event alone resulted in an estimated 22% loss of coral cover across the entire reef.

Trends & Issues Affecting the GBR

The reef faces compounding pressures: ocean warming drives bleaching, agricultural runoff fuels crown-of-thorns starfish outbreaks and algae overgrowth, cyclones cause acute physical damage, and ocean acidification degrades carbonate structures. Sea surface temperatures (SST) around the GBR have risen by approximately 1°C above pre-industrial levels, with projections indicating further increases under all emissions scenarios. Reef History & Decline

Coral reefs have existed in the region for at least 600,000 years, though the current reef structure formed predominantly over the last 8,000 years. Scientific monitoring began in earnest in the 1980s, revealing a dramatic long-term decline in hard coral cover. Early threats centred on overfishing and direct physical damage, but by the 2000s climate change had emerged as the dominant driver of decline — with mass bleaching events now occurring at a frequency that prevents full recovery between episodes.

Additionally, Crown of Thorns Starfish (COTS) benefit from overfishing and feast on live coral tissue. When these populations boom, the threat to coral reefs is significant, with a single COTS capable of consuming up to 10 square metres of coral per year. Outbreaks have been linked to agricultural runoff, which increases nutrient levels in the water and promotes the growth of phytoplankton that COTS larvae feed on. The combination of rising sea temperatures and increased nutrient runoff has led to more frequent and severe COTS outbreaks, further exacerbating the decline of coral cover on the GBR.

Analysing the Data

AIMS has done a fantastic job at monitoring the state of the reef over the last few decades, tracking key metrics including type of coral cover — soft, hard, the percentage of live and dead coral. The tracking of COTS, indicating periods of declining reef health, is inversely related to the population of coral trout, which protect the reef from preying COTS, maintaining a sustainable coral ecosystem.

What is this?

Hard coral is the living, calcified structure that builds the reef framework, while soft coral is more flexible but doesn't contribute to the growth of the reef's foundation. Soft coral provides shelter and habitats for marine life, and can be more resilient to environmental stressors. A shift from hard to soft coral dominance can indicate a stressed reef ecosystem, as hard corals are more vulnerable to bleaching and mortality under rising sea temperatures.

The ternary plot shows the relative proportions of hard coral, soft coral, and dead coral across reef sites over time. It is overlayed with the mean COTS per year, highlighting a trend in higher COTS populations being associated with a higher proportion of dead coral.

What is this?

Beyond the ecological data, there is much concern in the community about the survival of the reef. CSIRO, under the Social and Economic Long Term Monitoring Program (SELTMP), has been tracking public sentiment and awareness regarding the reef's health. The survey asks people for their perceptions about the threats facing the reef, what it means to them, and a variety of other questions.

The treemap (left) shows the words survey respondents chose to describe the reef — Beautiful, Coral, and Fish dominate, reflecting deep emotional attachment. The bubble chart (right) maps the threats they named most: Pollution and Climate Change dwarf all others. The conviction is clear — the reef is loved, and the threats are well understood. It is time for meaningful action to protect and restore this irreplaceable ecosystem for future generations.

Global Coral Reef Conditions (1980–2020)

The focus of this dashboard is the alarming rate of decline of the Great Barrier Reef. Unfortunately, the GBR is not alone in facing these challenges. Coral reefs worldwide are experiencing similar pressures and declines due to rising sea temperatures, pollution, overfishing — to name a few. This serves as a stark reminder of the urgent need for action to protect these vital ecosystems.

This interactive world map has 4 attributes: bleaching percentage, coral cover percentage, SST anomaly and COTS. Each dot marks an individual reef survey site and the intensity of the attribute is indicated by the colour intensity. Select a parameter from the dropdown and drag the year slider to explore how bleaching intensity, coral cover, sea surface temperature, and temperature anomalies have shifted across the world's reef systems over four decades.
